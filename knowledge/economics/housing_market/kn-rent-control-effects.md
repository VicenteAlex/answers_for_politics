## Scope
Country-agnostic synthesis of empirical evidence on rent control (rent regulation of private rental housing). Definitions used here:
- **First-generation** controls: nominal rent freezes or board-set rent levels (e.g. post-war Europe, Cambridge MA until 1995, Berlin Mietendeckel 2020-2021).
- **Second-generation** controls: caps linked to a reference (index, previous rent, inflation), often exempting new construction and allowing increases for renovation (Germany's Mietpreisbremse 2015, Catalonia 2020 and 2024, Paris).
- **Third-generation / tenancy rent control**: caps on increases within a tenancy only, with free rents between tenancies (much of the US; Spain's annual update caps on existing contracts, IRAV).
- **Stock vs flow**: controls on updates of existing contracts (stock) vs controls on the rent of new contracts (flow). Spain uses both: IRAV/2-3% caps on updates and, in zonas tensionadas, caps on new contracts.

## Mechanisms
1. Price cap → lower rent for covered tenants (direct transfer from landlords to sitting tenants).
2. Lower expected return on renting → owners exit to sale (often to owner-occupiers), own use, short-term/seasonal/room rental if unregulated, redevelopment, or vacancy → less rental supply. The size depends on the gap between capped and market rent and on the attractiveness of exit options.
3. Segmentation → demand displaced from the covered segment raises prices in uncontrolled segments (exempt units, neighbouring municipalities, low-price units, new contracts) → incumbents gain, searchers lose.
4. Lock-in → lower mobility and mismatch of households to dwellings.
5. Lower maintenance incentives → quality decline (more for strict caps).
6. Pricing-in of regulation risk → landlords set higher entry rents or require more guarantees; asset values can fall when regulation is expected to tighten.
7. Sale market: two opposing forces. (a) Units sold out of the rental sector add supply to the sale market and lower rental yields reduce investor demand, pushing sale prices down. (b) Renters who cannot find rentals try to buy, and investors may prefer owner-occupier sales, raising demand for owner-occupied housing. The net sign is empirical and context-specific.

## Evidence by claim
- **Covered rents fall** (strong): Catalonia 2020 -4% to -6% (register data) and about -5% (Monràs); Germany 2015 up to -5% fading after a year; Berlin freeze large drops in ad rents. Catalonia 2024: -3 pp in rent growth in a basic DiD that is not robust.
- **Supply falls** (moderate, contested for Catalonia): San Francisco -15%; Berlin sharp fall in ads; Catalonia 2020 split (Jofre-Monseny et al. find no change in total agreements or stock; Monràs and García-Montalvo find about -10% new contracts concentrated above the reference price); Catalonia 2024 about -13% agreements per capita (weak design) and -22% ads in Barcelona in descriptive listing data. Note the measurement issue: ads and new contracts fall both when owners withdraw units and when sitting tenants move less (lower turnover), which rent caps also cause.
- **Conversion to owner-occupation/sale** (moderate): directly shown in San Francisco; indirect elsewhere. No causal Spanish evidence yet on how many capped units were sold.
- **Uncontrolled rents rise** (moderate): Germany, Berlin neighbours, Catalonia's low-price segment.
- **Sale prices** (insufficient): Cambridge (controls lowered values), Germany (land values for exempt new building up), Berlin (price-to-rent ratio up during cap, then 10-15% below trend from regulatory risk). No causal estimate for Spain. Descriptively, INE house prices in Catalonia rose less than the Spanish average after March 2024 (see es-dossier-vivienda-alquiler), which does not support the idea that the caps pushed sale prices up faster there, but is not a causal test either.
- **Mobility, quality, insider/outsider incidence** (moderate): consistent across US and German evidence; Spanish descriptive data on entry rents and competition per ad point the same way.

## Open debates
- Supply effects of second-generation caps in Catalonia: data source (contract register vs new contracts by price segment vs listings) and window (pandemic) drive the disagreement.
- Whether lower ad counts reflect withdrawal or reduced turnover; requires stock data (active contracts, AEAT declarations) and tracking of units (cadastre, sales registers).
- Welfare: efficiency costs vs insurance/redistribution value (Favilukis et al.). Targeting matters; Spanish caps are not income-tested.
- Long-run effects on construction: most second-generation regimes exempt new building, but regulatory risk (Berlin) can still reduce construction.

## Transfer to Spain
Spain's 2024+ regime (Ley 12/2023) is second-generation (index-based cap on new contracts in declared areas, stronger for large landlords and units not rented in the previous five years) combined with caps on annual updates (IRAV). Closest evidence: Catalonia 2020 and 2024 studies, Germany 2015. Barcelona has a very inelastic supply, a large seasonal/tourist segment and strong demand, so displacement to unregulated segments (seasonal, rooms, sale) is plausible; Catalonia extended caps to seasonal and room rentals in 2025 for this reason. Evidence windows are short (≤ 2 years).
