    statement: A large share of jobs is exposed to generative AI (roughly 40% of employment worldwide and about 60% in advanced economies, about half of it with high complementarity), but measured effects on aggregate employment, hours and earnings up to 2025-26 are small or null.
    strength: moderate

  - claim_id: claim-ai-adjustment-falls-on-entrants
    statement: When technology does reduce employment in exposed jobs, the adjustment falls mainly on young entrants (fewer hires) rather than on incumbent workers, who tend to be retained or redeployed.
    strength: moderate
    consensus: divided
    scope: Robots in Germany (B-grade); US payroll data for generative AI 2022-2026 (descriptive, under revision); Denmark finds no effect overall.
  - claim_id: claim-robots-net-employment-depends-on-context
    statement: Industrial robots reduced employment and wages in more exposed US local labour markets, but in Germany, Europe and Spanish manufacturing firms displacement was offset by job creation elsewhere or in adopting firms; low-skilled and blue-collar workers lose relative ground.
    strength: moderate
    consensus: divided
    scope: 1990-2016 robot diffusion; shift-share and firm-level designs; transfer to AI uncertain because AI affects cognitive, white-collar tasks.
  - claim_id: claim-genai-productivity-gains-largest-for-novices

    strength: moderate
    consensus: majority
    scope: Field evidence from customer support; macro estimate depends on assumed task shares and cost savings.
  - claim_id: claim-epl-slows-reallocation-and-risky-adoption
    statement: Strict dismissal protection for regular contracts lowers hiring and job reallocation and slows productivity growth and the size of risky, technology-intensive sectors, while protecting incumbents; rules on temporary contracts show weaker effects on productivity.
    strength: moderate
    consensus: majority

  - claim_id: claim-short-time-work-fits-temporary-not-structural-shocks
    statement: Short-time work schemes (ERTE, Kurzarbeit, cassa integrazione) save jobs at moderate cost in large temporary shocks, but under persistent or structural shocks they keep workers in declining firms, slow reallocation and lose effectiveness with duration.
    strength: moderate
    consensus: majority
    scope: Italy, France, Germany, Spain (COVID ERTE); AI displacement is more likely to be structural than cyclical.
  - claim_id: claim-training-pays-in-medium-run
    statement: Active labour market programmes based on training have small effects in the short run and larger positive effects 2-3 years later; retraining of displaced workers raises earnings but gains can fade over a decade. Programme design matters more than context.
    strength: strong
    consensus: majority

## Scope

Synthesis for the question "can labour laws change how AI/automation affects workers?". Two blocks: (1) how large and how fast displacement is; (2) which institutions change who bears the cost. Spanish legislation is described in `entries/es/measures` (RDL 3/2012, RDL 32/2021, RDL 9/2021, Ley 3/2023, SMI decrees, AI Act).

## 1. Size and timing of displacement

- **Exposure is not displacement.** Task-based indices (Eloundou et al.; Felten et al.; IMF; ILO; OECD) measure the share of tasks an AI could perform or assist, not job losses. The IMF splits advanced-economy exposure (about 60%) into high-complementarity (27%) and low-complementarity (33%) jobs; the ILO 2025 index puts only 3.3% of global employment in the top exposure category. For Spain, BBVA Research (2026) finds just over 30% of employment in medium-high or high exposure occupations, with employment growing in all groups since 2022.
- **Causal effects so far are small.** Denmark (Humlum & Vestergaard) rules out earnings or hours effects above 2% two years after ChatGPT. The main counter-signal is descriptive: Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen report a 13-19% relative employment fall for 22-25 year olds in the most exposed US occupations (revised several times; not causal).
- **Robots as precedent.** US local labour markets lost employment and wages (Acemoglu & Restrepo); Germany absorbed displacement through fewer entrants and service jobs (Dauth et al.); Spanish adopting firms grew (Koch et al.). The precedent suggests the incidence falls on entrants and the low-skilled, and depends on demand and institutions.
- **Productivity:** large task-level gains, concentrated among novices (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond); modest macro TFP gains in Acemoglu's estimate. The two together imply slow aggregate change unless adoption and complementary investment accelerate.

## 2. Do institutions change the impact?

Yes, mainly through **who** bears the adjustment and **how fast** reallocation happens, rather than through the total amount of technological change:

- **Dismissal costs** protect incumbents and slow both destruction and creation; they discourage risky, technology-intensive activity (Bartelsman et al.; Bassanini et al.; Autor et al.). Under a structural shock, strict protection shifts the burden toward entrants and temporary workers (Spain's dual market is the classic case).
- **Short-time work** is insurance for temporary shocks; it hinders reallocation under persistent shocks (Giupponi & Landais), and in Spain the probability of returning to work fell with ERTE duration (Izquierdo et al. 2022). Spain's Mecanismo RED sectoral modality (2021), which ties support to retraining, is closer to what the evidence suggests for structural change, but has no evaluation.
- **Training/ALMP:** positive but delayed effects (Card, Kluve & Weber); design matters (Kluve). Retraining displaced workers raises earnings, with fading returns (Hyman).
- **Unemployment insurance:** small disincentive costs, possible match-quality gains; displacement losses are large and persistent, so insurance has high value (Jacobson et al.; Davis & von Wachter).
- **Minimum wage:** raises the incentive to automate low-skill routine tasks (Lordan & Neumark); Spanish 2019 evidence shows employment losses among affected workers of differing size. Generative AI mainly exposes higher-wage cognitive tasks, so this channel is less central for AI than for robots.
- **Worker voice:** associated with protecting insiders, possibly at outsiders' expense; codetermination did not reduce investment (Jäger et al.). Information rights on algorithms (Spain art. 64.4.d ET; AI Act art. 26(7)) have no evaluation yet.
- **Robot/AI taxes:** theory supports at most small, temporary taxes or removing capital-favouring tax distortions.
- **Working time cuts** have not created jobs in France or Portugal; they are not a demonstrated tool against technological unemployment.

## Gaps

No causal study on how employment protection affects AI adoption; no evaluation of Mecanismo RED or of algorithmic-transparency rights; Spanish displacement-loss estimates by cause are missing; AI evidence covers only 2023-2026.
