## Scope
Public-finance, demographic (pensions), housing and integration effects of immigration in high-income countries, with Spain-specific evidence where available. Labour-market effects: kn-immigration-labor-market-effects. Crime, enforcement, borders and politics: kn-immigration-crime-legal-status-enforcement.

## Mechanisms
- **Fiscal**: contributions depend on employment and wages (income tax, social contributions, VAT); costs on age (education of children, health and pensions in old age), household composition and benefit eligibility. Pure public goods (defence, debt interest) are not increased by an extra resident; whether they are allocated per capita changes results.
- **Life-cycle**: working-age arrivals are net contributors while working and net recipients in retirement; static snapshots of a young immigrant population flatter the result, dynamic projections less so.
- **Pensions**: in a pay-as-you-go system, more contributors today ease current finances, but each contributor earns future rights; the net gain depends on whether immigrants' contribution careers and wages are long and high enough.
- **Housing**: more households -> more demand; prices/rents rise if supply is inelastic (land, permits), construction rises if elastic.
- **Integration**: language, recognition of qualifications, legal status and residential segregation affect employment and children's outcomes.

## Evidence by claim
**claim-imm-fis-net-position-small (moderate).** OECD (2021): about -1% to +1% of GDP in most of 25 countries over 2006-2018; age explains much of the variation; equalising employment rates would raise contributions by more than 1/3 point of GDP in about 30% of countries. Static accounting, so graded C.

**claim-imm-fis-depends-on-skills-and-age (moderate).** UK: recent EEA immigrants paid about 34% more than they received (2001-2011), while non-EEA immigrants were cumulatively negative over 1995-2011 (Dustmann & Frattini 2014). US: first generation costlier than natives (mainly local education spending), second generation among strongest contributors (NASEM 2017).

**claim-imm-fis-regularisation-raises-revenue (moderate, one study).** Spain 2005: about EUR 4,000 more tax revenue per regularised immigrant, no evidence of higher public spending (Elias et al. 2025).

**claim-imm-dem-delays-not-solves-ageing (moderate).** EU Ageing Report scenarios analysed by BBVA Research: demography adds 10.4 GDP points of pension spending to 2050; immigration (foreign-born rising to 28% of the population) eases only transitorily. Banco de España: population grows only through immigration, but projected flows do not offset ageing, which is more intense in Spain than in Germany, France or Italy. Both are grade D (projections/descriptive).

**claim-imm-hou-raises-housing-demand-prices (moderate).** Spain 1998-2008: immigration explains roughly one third of the housing boom in prices and construction (González & Ortega 2013; magnitudes vary across versions).

**claim-imm-int-second-generation-mobility (weak for Europe).** US evidence of strong upward mobility of children of immigrants (Abramitzky et al. 2021). Spain lacks comparable long-run linked data; the 2000s second generation is only now entering adulthood.

## Open debates
- Static vs dynamic accounting and allocation of public goods (sign can flip).
- Whether low-skill immigration in Spain raises or lowers long-run GDP per capita (employment gains vs productivity effects; Banco de España calls the productivity response highly uncertain).
- Housing: the relative role of immigration vs. household formation, tourism and supply constraints in the 2015-2026 price rise (not resolved with causal Spanish evidence for the recent period).
- Integration outcomes under restrictive vs. inclusive legal-status regimes.

## Transfer to Spain
- Spain combines rapid ageing with large recent inflows: foreign-nationality residents 7.26 million and foreign-born 10.04 million on 1 Jan 2026 out of 49.6 million (indicators es-population-foreign-nationality, es-population-foreign-born, es-population).
- Foreign workers affiliated to Social Security: 3.55 million in August 2026, 15.9% of affiliates (Ministerio de Inclusión, see es-dossier-inmigracion).
- No recent official Spanish fiscal-incidence study by nationality was located (AIReF/Banco de España); this is a key evidence gap for claims either way.
- Proposals of "prioridad nacional" in access to social housing and benefits (Vox 2026; PP-Vox Extremadura agreement 2026) and of restricting benefits by legal status (PP 2026) act on the expenditure side; their fiscal size is unknown and their legality is disputed (constitutional and EU equal-treatment limits acknowledged by the PP 2026 program itself).
