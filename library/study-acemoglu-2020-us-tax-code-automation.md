## Question
Does the US tax code tax capital (including automation equipment and software) less than labor, and if so, does this induce excessive automation and lower employment?

## Method and data
Computes effective tax rates on labor and on capital in the US over time, then uses a task-based model in which firms choose whether to automate tasks to compute the optimal tax structure and the employment and labor-share effects of reforms.

## Findings (abstract)
- The US tax system is biased against labor and in favor of capital, and has become more so in recent years; this promotes automation beyond the socially desirable level.
- Moving from the 2010s US tax system to optimal taxation of capital and labor would raise employment by 4.02 percent and the labor share by 0.78 percentage point, and restore the optimal level of automation.
- If optimal taxes are infeasible, more modest reforms can still increase employment by 1.14-1.96 percent, and then an additional automation tax is beneficial, because marginal automated tasks bring little productivity gain but displace workers.
- Reducing labor taxes, or combining lower capital taxes with automation taxes, increases employment much more than the uniform capital-tax cuts enacted between 2000 and 2018.

## Limitations
- Model-based (grade C); results depend on the elasticity of substitution, the productivity of marginal automation and effective-tax-rate measurement.
- US-specific tax parameters. BPEA is a conference journal with published discussant comments rather than standard anonymous refereeing.
- A Brookings summary cites different scenario figures (6.5 percent employment, 1.1 points labor share); this card uses only the abstract figures.

## Relevance
In Spain, labour bears high social security contributions while investment incentives (accelerated depreciation, R&D credits) lower the cost of capital. The mechanism "lower relative tax on machines → more automation of marginal tasks → less employment" suggests that the relative tax treatment of labour and capital matters for how an AI shock affects employment; Spain-specific effective rates are not estimated here.
