## Question
What are the plausible macroeconomic effects of AI on productivity, wages and inequality over the next decade?

## Method and data
Task-based model: by Hulten's theorem, aggregate TFP gains ≈ share of tasks affected × average task-level cost savings. Calibrated with exposure estimates (Eloundou et al.) and experimental productivity gains (e.g. Brynjolfsson et al.; Noy & Zhang), adjusted for profitability of automation and for hard-to-learn tasks.

## Findings (abstract)
- No more than a 0.66% increase in TFP over 10 years.
- Adjusting for harder-to-learn tasks (early evidence comes from easy tasks): less than 0.53%.
- AI is unlikely to increase inequality as much as previous automation, but there is no evidence it will reduce labour-income inequality; it is predicted to widen the gap between capital and labour income.
- Mentions possible negative social value of some AI uses (e.g. manipulation).

## Limitations
- Grade C: structural calibration; results are as good as the exposure shares and task-level savings fed in. Excludes new tasks/products and faster capability gains, which critics argue bias the estimate down.
- A MIT summary cites 0.71% for a different version; figures here are from the published abstract.

## Relevance
Sets a low benchmark for productivity (and therefore for both displacement speed and income gains). Spain-specific projections (Funcas 2026) cite this estimate.
