## Question
Did rent control depress the value of neighbouring never-controlled properties, and what happened to property values when it ended?

## Method and data
A 1995 Massachusetts referendum abruptly ended rent control in Cambridge, a strict regime with board-set rents. The authors compare properties with different exposure to formerly controlled units nearby, using administrative assessments and all residential transactions 1988-2005.

## Findings
- Decontrol produced large, positive and robust spillovers onto never-controlled properties near decontrolled units.
- Between 1994 and 2004 decontrol added about $1.8 billion to the value of Cambridge's housing stock, nearly a quarter of the city's residential price appreciation in that period; more than half of the induced appreciation came from spillovers to never-controlled properties.
- Residential investment explains only a small fraction of the total effect.

## Limitations
Strict, long-lived US regime; removal effects need not mirror introduction effects. Higher property values benefit owners and are not by themselves welfare gains for tenants, whose outcomes are not measured.

## Relevance
On the sale-price channel, this evidence points in the opposite direction to the hypothesis that rent control raises house prices: in Cambridge, controls were associated with lower values of controlled and nearby uncontrolled housing, and removing them raised values. Transfer to Spain is limited by the strictness and duration of the Cambridge regime.
