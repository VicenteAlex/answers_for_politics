## Question
Which firms does short-time work (STW) actually help, and how much does a saved job cost?

## Method and data
Model predicting that STW saves jobs only in firms with severe revenue shocks, while milder-shock firms mainly reduce hours. Tested with administrative data on all French establishments; firms grouped by quintile of predicted hours growth. The scheme is administered by the 95 départements of metropolitan France; identification details are in the paper and are not summarised here.

## Findings
Abstract (primary): STW saved jobs and raised hours in firms hit by large negative shocks; these firms recovered quickly after the recession. Windfall effects significantly raised the cost per job saved, but STW was still more cost-efficient at saving jobs than wage subsidies.
Authors' VoxEU column (secondary, numbers as stated):
- Bottom quintile (largest predicted hours fall): one additional enrolled worker saves 0.6 jobs in 2009; a 1-point subsidy (share of previous-year payroll) raised employment by about 20% and hours by about 16%.
- Cost per job saved in the bottom quintile: EUR 2,700 in 2009, 7% of average annual labour costs. Counting subsidies to all quintiles: 45% of average annual labour costs per job.
- French take-up peaked at 33% of employees at the end of May 2020 (OECD average 21%).

## Limitations
Working paper, not peer reviewed at the time of access; numbers taken from the authors' column.

## Relevance
Targeting matters: STW is cost-effective for firms with large, temporary revenue shocks and largely a windfall elsewhere. A diffuse AI/automation shock that gradually reduces labour demand looks more like the "mild, persistent" case, where STW mainly subsidises hours reductions without saving jobs.
