## Question
Did a sudden, large, mostly low-skilled immigrant inflow (about 125,000 Cubans in 1980) depress wages or raise unemployment of incumbent workers in Miami?

## Method and data
Difference-in-differences comparison of Miami with four comparison cities (Atlanta, Houston, Los Angeles, Tampa-St. Petersburg) using CPS microdata before and after 1980. The inflow was driven by a Cuban political decision, not by Miami's labour demand, which makes it a classic natural experiment.

## Findings
- The boatlift increased Miami's labour force by about 7%, and by more in less-skilled occupations and industries (abstract).
- Virtually no effect on wages or unemployment rates of less-skilled workers, whites, blacks or earlier Cuban immigrants.
- Card attributes fast absorption to Miami's history of absorbing earlier immigrant waves (industry structure adapted to low-skill labour).

## Limitations
- Small CPS samples for Miami subgroups; standard errors are large.
- Comparison cities chosen by the author; the choice of controls and subgroups is the crux of the later Borjas (2017) vs Peri & Yasenov (2019) dispute.
- One city, one episode; refugees in an expanding local economy.
- Card's abstract page read; the full text was not re-opened for this card.

## Relevance
Benchmark case for the claim that large low-skill inflows need not depress native wages. Transfer to Spain is partial: Spain's 2000-2008 inflow was larger relative to population and more spread geographically (see study-gonzalez-2011-spanish-regions-immigration-flows).
