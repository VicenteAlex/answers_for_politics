## Question
Do mortgage credit expansion policies for buyers improve affordability, or are they capitalised into prices? Does it depend on supply elasticity?

## Method and data
Help to Buy (HtB) equity loans for new-build homes in England, from 2013. Two spatial discontinuities: (i) the Greater London boundary, where the equity loan cap rose from 20% to 40% in 2016; (ii) the English/Welsh border, where scheme parameters differed. Difference-in-discontinuities on prices and construction.

## Findings
- Greater London (severely supply-constrained, unaffordable): HtB significantly increased house prices, by more than the expected present value of the implied interest subsidy (working paper), with no detectable effect on construction volumes.
- English/Welsh border (affordable, laxer supply): HtB increased construction numbers without a noticeable effect on prices.
- Bunching of new units below the price threshold and a shift toward smaller units (working paper).
- HtB did not help would-be buyers in already unaffordable areas but improved the financial performance of participating developers.
- Point estimates were not verified (abstracts report directions only).

## Limitations
Local estimates at boundaries; HtB is an equity loan for new builds only, whereas Spain's ICO line is a public guarantee on the top 20% of a mortgage for any eligible first home (mostly second-hand stock) with price caps. Magnitudes depend on the size of the scheme relative to the market.

## Relevance
Closest well-identified evidence for the Spanish ICO first-home guarantee line: the expected effect depends on local supply elasticity, so in tight markets (Madrid, Barcelona, Balearics, Málaga) part of the support may be capitalised into prices. The Spanish line is small (about 1.1 billion euros of guaranteed lending to October 2025), which limits aggregate price effects.
