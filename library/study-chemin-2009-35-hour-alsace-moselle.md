## Question
Did France's 35-hour workweek (Aubry laws, from 1998) increase employment, as intended by work-sharing arguments?

## Method and data
For historical reasons linked to the return of Alsace-Moselle to France in 1918, the region has local laws that made the 35-hour reform less stringent there. The authors use the region as a less-treated group in difference-in-differences and triple-difference designs comparing hours and employment with the rest of France.

## Findings (abstract)
- Double and triple differences confirm that implementation of the 35-hour workweek was less stringent in Alsace-Moselle.
- Yet there is no significant difference in employment between Alsace-Moselle and the rest of France.
- The authors conclude that this casts doubt on the effectiveness of the regulation (i.e. on its job-creation effect).

## Limitations
- Identifies the effect of a difference in treatment intensity, not of the full reform vs no reform; a null may reflect low power for a small intensity gap.
- One region; spillovers between Alsace-Moselle and neighbouring labour markets are possible.
- The French reform came with payroll-tax reductions and wage moderation agreements, which differ from a pure hours mandate. Abowd's published comment notes that the best estimation strategy for the 39-to-35 change remains unclear.

## Relevance
Relevant to Spain's 2025 debate on reducing the statutory workweek to 37.5 hours. Combined with Crépon and Kramarz (2002) and Raposo and van Ours (2010), the evidence does not support the claim that mandated hours reductions create jobs through work-sharing. Proposals to share remaining work after AI-driven displacement through shorter hours lack support from these episodes.
