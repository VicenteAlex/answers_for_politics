## Question
Did France's mandatory reduction of the standard workweek from 40 to 39 hours (1 February 1982), with monthly earnings of minimum-wage workers held constant, create or destroy jobs for the workers directly affected?

## Method and data
After Mitterrand's election in 1981 the minimum wage rose 5 percent; then the workweek was cut by one hour while monthly earnings of minimum-wage earners were kept stable (an hourly wage increase). The authors compare job-loss probabilities between 1981 and 1982 of workers employed 40 hours or more in March 1981 (treated) with workers employed 36-39 hours (control), using individual French worker data (data source not checked against the full text).

## Findings (abstract)
- Workers employed 40 hours and above in March 1981 were more likely to lose their jobs between 1981 and 1982 than workers employed 36-39 hours.
- Many workers were still working 40 hours after February 1982; they were also strongly affected.
- Estimated impact of the one-hour reduction on employment losses: between 2 percent and 4 percent, depending on methodology or data.
- Minimum-wage workers were most affected.

## Limitations
- The reduction was bundled with a rise in hourly labour cost for minimum-wage workers, so the effect mixes hours and wage-cost channels.
- Short horizon (one year) and only directly affected incumbents; aggregate employment and hiring are not measured.
- Working-paper versions report somewhat different job-loss probabilities; only the abstract figures are used here.

## Relevance
Spain debated a reduction of the statutory workweek from 40 to 37.5 hours without wage cuts in 2025. This study shows that a mandated hours cut that raises hourly labour cost can raise job loss for low-wage workers, a channel that would add to automation incentives in an AI shock.
