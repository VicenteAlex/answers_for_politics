## Question
How did German local labour markets and individual workers adjust to the rise of industrial robots?

## Method and data
Detailed administrative employer-employee data; local robot exposure predicted by a shift-share variable (instrumented with robot adoption in other countries); worker-level panels following incumbents.

## Findings (abstract)
- Robot exposure is linked to displacement in manufacturing, but this is fully offset by new jobs in services.
- Job losses fall mostly on young workers entering the labour force; incumbents experience more stable employment, driven by taking over new tasks in their original plants, with new jobs of higher quality on several measures.
- Young workers shift from vocational training toward college/university.
- Workers in complementary occupations (managers, technical scientists) benefit.
- The earlier CEPR version (2017) reported that one robot replaces about two manufacturing jobs, roughly 23% of the 1994-2014 manufacturing employment decline (secondary, not in the JEEA abstract).

## Limitations
- Grade B: shift-share IV on rich worker data; same identifying assumptions as Acemoglu & Restrepo.
- The role of works councils/unions in the "no layoffs, fewer hires" adjustment is not stated in the abstract; it was discussed by an author in interviews (secondary). Not verified as a tested result.

## Relevance
Shows adjustment can run through reduced hiring of young entrants rather than layoffs: aggregate employment can stay flat while entry-level opportunities shrink. Spain's high youth unemployment and dual contracts make the entry margin relevant. German institutions (employment protection, co-determination) are a candidate moderator, but not tested here.
