## Question
What happens to tenants, landlords and the rental stock when an existing rent control regime (cap on increases within a tenancy) is extended to buildings that were previously exempt?

## Method and data
A 1994 ballot measure extended San Francisco rent control to small multifamily buildings built before 1980, while comparable buildings built in 1980 or later stayed uncontrolled. The authors compare buildings on either side of the construction-year cutoff (difference-in-differences) using new data that track individuals' addresses over time, plus parcel data on conversions and redevelopment.

## Findings
- Tenancy stability: rent control reduced renters' mobility by about 20% and reduced displacement from San Francisco (abstract).
- Supply: landlords of treated buildings reduced rental housing supply by about 15%, mainly by selling to owner-occupants (condominium conversion) and by redeveloping buildings (abstract).
- Market effect: the authors conclude that the lost rental supply likely pushed up market rents over time, working against the law's goals; rent control protects incumbent renters in the short run.
- Distribution: the conversion to owner-occupied housing is associated with higher-income residents (gentrification channel), per the paper's framing of inequality effects.

## Limitations
Specific US regime (within-tenancy caps, vacancy decontrol, legal conversion routes). The citywide rent effect is inferred from the supply loss, not directly estimated. Single city.

## Relevance
Leading causal evidence for the channel "rent control leads owners to sell to owner-occupiers". It concerns conversion of controlled rental units into owner-occupied units; it does not estimate the effect on house sale prices. Transfer to Spain is partial: Spanish controls (Ley 12/2023, zonas tensionadas) cap rents at signing by reference to an index or prior rent; selling to owner-occupiers is also a legal exit option in Spain.
