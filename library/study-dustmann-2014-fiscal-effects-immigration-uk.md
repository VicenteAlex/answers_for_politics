## Question
Do immigrants to the UK pay more in taxes than they receive in benefits and public services, and how does this differ by origin and arrival cohort?

## Method and data
Static accounting allocating revenue and expenditure items to immigrant groups and natives, yearly 1995-2011, using LFS microdata; separate estimates for EEA vs non-EEA and for arrivals since 2000.

## Findings
- Recent (post-1999) EEA immigrants contributed about 34% more than they took out in 2001-2011 (net about GBP 22.1bn), per the working paper and press release.
- Over 1995-2011 the overall net contribution of natives was negative; non-EEA immigrants' cumulative net contribution was negative (about GBP 104bn per the authors' VoxEU column) while EEA immigrants' was positive.
- Recent immigrants of both groups were less likely to receive benefits and live in social housing than natives.

## Limitations
- Static accounting; results depend on allocation of public goods and on a period with budget deficits (when most groups are net negative).
- Figures come from the press release, column and abstract; the journal tables were not opened.

## Relevance
Shows that fiscal effects depend strongly on immigrants' education and employment (EEA vs non-EEA). For Spain, the composition of inflows (Latin America, Morocco, EU) and their employment rates are the analogous drivers.
