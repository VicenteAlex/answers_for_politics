## Question
How do native wages and employment respond to a sudden, policy-driven inflow of foreign workers into a local labour market?

## Method and data
After the fall of the Iron Curtain, Czech workers were allowed to commute (not reside) to German border municipalities. The inflow fell sharply with distance from the border, so nearby vs. farther municipalities are compared using German social-security records.

## Findings (abstract, no numbers retrieved)
- Native wages fall moderately; native employment falls sharply.
- Younger natives bear larger wage losses, older natives larger employment losses.
- Most of the employment loss comes from fewer natives entering local jobs ("outsiders" shielding "insiders"), not from out-migration or exits to non-employment.

## Limitations
- Commuters, not settled immigrants: they spend income elsewhere, so the local demand effect that usually offsets supply shocks is weaker than with resident immigrants.
- Specific institutional setting (German wage rigidity in the 1990s).

## Relevance
Strong counter-evidence to the "no effect" view in a European, rigid-wage setting. Relevant to Spain's segmented labour market (temporary contracts, collective bargaining), where adjustment may also fall on entry and employment rather than wages.
