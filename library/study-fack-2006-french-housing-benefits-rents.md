## Question
Who captures demand-side rent subsidies: tenants or landlords?

## Method and data
Natural experiment from a French reform that extended housing benefits to households previously not eligible, comparing rents of newly eligible low-income households with other groups before and after the reform. Reform years, data and specification were not checked against the full text (abstract only).

## Findings
- One additional euro of housing benefit increased the rent paid by new claimants by about 0.78 euros (abstract).
- The reform raised demand from low-income households and from students who used the benefit to live independently, while supply did not respond in the short or medium term.
- The only likely real effect was a small increase in housing quality.
- The author attributes the large pass-through to a very low housing supply elasticity.

## Limitations
Abstract-level verification only. Estimates come from one reform in one period; pass-through partly reflects quality upgrades. The subsidy was large and broad (many students), which may raise incidence relative to narrow, capped programmes.

## Relevance
Main reference for the expected incidence of Spain's Bono Alquiler Joven (250 euros/month, ages 18-35, rent caps of 600-900 euros) in tight urban markets. Supports the mechanism "demand subsidy + inelastic supply -> higher rents". Does not by itself predict Spanish magnitudes (the Spanish bono is capped and smaller relative to the market).
