## Question
What are the employment and welfare effects of subsidising hours reductions (short-time work, STW) in a recession, and when is it efficient?

## Method and data
Italian administrative social security data; quasi-experimental variation from STW eligibility rules that differ across firms. Firm-level, worker-level (matched against laid-off workers) and local-labour-market analyses; a model to translate estimates into welfare.

## Findings
Abstract (primary):
- Large, significant negative effects on hours and large positive effects on headcount employment.
- Adverse selection of low-productivity firms into STW reduces its long-run insurance value; with persistent shocks it creates significant negative reallocation effects.
Authors' VoxEU column (secondary, numbers as stated):
- Treated firms: "a 40% reduction in hours worked per employee" and "a 40% increase in the number of employees", with no discernible effect on wage rates.
- The subsidy lasts up to one year; two years after treatment there are no significant differences in employment probability, earnings or total income relative to laid-off workers.
- Bottom-productivity firms were "three times more likely" to take up STW, with significantly smaller employment effects.
- STW lowers employment growth of non-treated firms in the same local labour market and has a small negative effect on local TFP growth.
- Without STW, unemployment during the Great Recession would have been "almost 2% higher" and TFP "about 2% higher", at a welfare cost of "about 2%".

## Limitations
Italy's recession was long; external validity to short, sharp shocks (COVID) or other designs is partial.

## Relevance
Key for an AI/automation shock: such a shock is structural, not temporary. The evidence indicates STW then mostly delays separations and slows reallocation toward expanding firms. Spain's ERTE and the RED mechanism (sectoral modality, 2021 reform) face the same trade-off.
