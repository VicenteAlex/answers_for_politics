## Question
Should robots (automation capital) be taxed when automation lowers the wages of routine workers, given that the government can also use income taxes?

## Method and data
Quantitative overlapping-generations model with technical progress in automation and endogenous choice of routine vs non-routine skills, calibrated to the US. Computes the optimal tax system (Mirrleesian and restricted versions) and compares it with the current US tax system.

## Findings
- Under the current US tax system, a sustained fall in automation costs can lead to a massive rise in income inequality (abstract).
- It is optimal to tax robots while the current generations of routine workers, who can no longer move to non-routine occupations, are active in the labour force; once these workers retire, optimal robot taxes are zero (abstract).
- In the NBER revision checked, the optimal robot tax is 7 percent in the first decade, 3 percent in the second and 1 percent in the third, then zero. The Mirrleesian optimal-taxation section of the same text reports robot taxes of 8.5 percent (1988-1997), 3.1 percent (1998-2007) and 0.8 percent in the following decade. These are model outputs, not empirical estimates.
- Rationale differs from the "replace lost payroll revenue" argument: the robot tax is a redistribution device that compresses wages when income taxes cannot target routine workers directly.

## Limitations
- Results depend on calibration and on restrictions on the tax instruments: the authors note that if the tax system allowed different lump-sum taxes on different workers, technical progress would always be welfare improving (gains could be redistributed directly).
- Defining and measuring "robots" for tax purposes is not addressed empirically.
- Grade C: structural model.

## Relevance
For Spain and an AI shock, the model argues that any tax on automation should be temporary and targeted at protecting cohorts already locked into routine skills, while training and skill choice of younger cohorts remove the case for it. It complements Acemoglu, Manera and Restrepo (2020), which argues that current tax codes already subsidise automation relative to labour.
