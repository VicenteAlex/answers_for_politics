## Question
What were the first observable effects of the 2012 labour reform (decentralised bargaining, firm opt-outs, redefined economic dismissal, severance for unfair dismissal cut to 33 days per year with a 24-month cap, no administrative authorisation for collective dismissals) on wages, employment and job flows?

## Method and data
Residuals from wage equations (unemployment, inflation, productivity) and from employment/unemployment-activity equations before and after the reform; descriptive analysis of collective agreements, opt-outs (ECL) and EPA flows by contract type. No control group.

## Findings (full text)
- Agreed wage rises: 1.3% in 2012 (more than one point below previous December inflation); 0.6% in the first eight months of 2013; 0.4% in newly signed agreements. Negative wage-equation residuals point to wage moderation beyond fundamentals.
- Opt-outs stayed at around 5%-6% of employees; firms accounting for 12.1% of employment used substantial modification of working conditions in 2012, concentrated in large firms.
- Employment: "a certain positive impact on net employment", associated with wage moderation, "although the evidence is less conclusive".
- Temporary employment ratio fell to 23% in 2013 Q2, attributed to the cycle; no significant progress in the composition of flows, still dominated by temporary workers. The new entrepreneurs' contract was around 7% of new permanent contracts.

## Limitations
Very short post-reform window, deep recession and fiscal consolidation confound effects; authors stress high uncertainty (grade D).

## Relevance
Shows that lowering dismissal costs on permanent contracts in Spain operated mainly through wages and internal flexibility in the short run, without reducing duality. For an AI shock, internal flexibility (hours, tasks, pay) is an adjustment margin that can substitute for dismissals when the shock is firm-specific.
