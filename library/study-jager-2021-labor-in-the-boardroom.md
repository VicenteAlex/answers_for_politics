## Question
Does giving workers one-third of corporate board seats (board-level codetermination) raise wages, change the wage structure or reduce investment through hold-up?

## Method and data
A 1994 German reform abolished the one-third board-seat mandate for stock corporations incorporated after August 1994, while older cohorts stayed covered. The authors compare firm cohorts incorporated just before and after the cutoff, and use limited liability companies (not affected by the reform) as a robustness check.

## Findings
- No effects of board-level codetermination on wages and the wage structure, even in firms with particularly flexible wages (abstract).
- No effect on rent sharing or on the labor share (abstract).
- Hold-up/disinvestment is rejected: "shared governance, if anything, increases capital formation" (abstract). The NBER Digest summary of the working paper reports that firms with shared governance had 40 to 50 percent larger long-term capital stocks (not checked in the published tables).

## Limitations
- Applies to one-third (minority) board representation in a cohort of mostly young stock corporations; parity codetermination in large firms (>2,000 employees) is not identified.
- Effects on employment protection, job design or technology adoption are not the main outcomes.
- Germany's broader system (works councils, sectoral bargaining) may condition the result.

## Relevance
Spain has no board-level codetermination (Art. 129.2 of the Constitution mandates promoting worker participation in firms, without implementing legislation). The evidence suggests minority worker board seats neither raise wages nor deter capital investment; for an AI/automation shock, it implies worker voice at board level is unlikely to block capital deepening, while its effects on how technology is deployed remain unmeasured here.
