## Question
How does a wealth tax affect the accumulation of taxable wealth among the rich, in the short and the long run?

## Method and data
Danish third-party-reported administrative wealth data. The 1989 reform reduced the wealth tax for moderately wealthy households (higher exemption threshold) while the very wealthy kept facing it; the authors compare wealth trajectories of more and less affected groups. They calibrate a life-cycle model with a bequest motive to the quasi-experimental moments and simulate long-run effects.

## Findings
- Clear reduced-form effects of wealth taxes on taxable wealth in the short and medium run, larger for the very wealthy than for the moderately wealthy (abstract).
- The calibrated model implies a long-run elasticity of taxable wealth with respect to the net-of-tax return that is "sizable at the top of the distribution" (abstract; point estimates not checked for this card).
- IEF (2026) attributes to this paper a decomposition in which, per extra unit of wealth-tax revenue, about 0.22 is lost through migration and 0.54 through intensive-margin responses (saving, investment, avoidance, evasion). Not verified against the paper.

## Limitations
- Denmark has extensive third-party reporting, limiting evasion; responses can be larger where reporting is weaker.
- Long-run figures depend on a structural model.
- Real saving responses and avoidance are not fully separated.

## Relevance
Core reference for the claim that taxable wealth at the top is elastic to wealth taxes. Spain's IP relies on third-party data for financial assets, but exempts qualifying business assets and has a combined income-wealth cap, which Spanish evidence links to avoidance responses.
