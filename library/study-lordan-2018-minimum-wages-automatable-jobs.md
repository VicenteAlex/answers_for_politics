## Question
Do minimum wage increases accelerate the substitution of low-skilled workers by machines in jobs intensive in automatable (routine) tasks?

## Method and data
CPS 1980-2015. Automatable occupations defined from Autor and Dorn (2013) and Autor et al. (2015). Panel of state (urban/non-urban) × industry × year cells: the share of automatable employment held by low-skilled workers is regressed on the state minimum wage with fixed effects. Individual-level models estimate transitions out of automatable jobs into nonemployment or other jobs.

## Findings
- Raising the minimum wage significantly decreases the share of automatable employment held by low-skilled workers; the elasticity of this share with respect to the minimum wage is −0.10 (working-paper text).
- Effects are larger in manufacturing (elasticity −0.18), where the decline is sharpest for older workers, and also large for women and Black workers.
- Individual level: in manufacturing, a 10 percent minimum wage increase implies declines in the probability of employment of 0.25 percentage point (oldest group) and 0.22 percentage point (youngest group) for workers in automatable jobs; implied elasticities of becoming nonemployed −0.28 and −0.17.
- Some evidence of improved job opportunities for higher-skilled workers.

## Limitations
- Numbers above are from the NBER working paper (rev. Jan 2018); the published version may differ slightly.
- Two-way fixed-effects state panel; identification relies on state minimum wage timing being unrelated to industry-specific automation trends.
- "Automatable" is a task-based occupation proxy, not observed adoption of machines.

## Relevance
For Spain, where the SMI rose sharply from 2019, the mechanism "higher wage floor → stronger incentive to automate routine low-wage tasks" implies that an AI/automation shock and minimum-wage policy interact: displacement can be concentrated among older low-skilled workers in routine jobs, a group not covered by teen-focused minimum-wage evidence.
