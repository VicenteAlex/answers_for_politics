## Question
Is there a case for addressing wealth inequality through net wealth taxes, and how should they be designed?

## Method and data
Review of theory, empirical literature and country experiences; comparison with capital income and inheritance taxes.

## Findings
- 12 OECD countries had net wealth taxes in 1990; 4 levied recurrent taxes on individuals' net wealth in 2017. Revenues, with few exceptions, were very low.
- Repeals were justified by efficiency and administrative concerns and the observation that the taxes often failed their redistributive goals.
- Strong case for addressing wealth inequality through the tax system and for inheritance taxation; limited arguments for a net wealth tax in addition to broad-based capital income taxes and well-designed inheritance/gift taxes, because wealth taxes ignore actual returns and tend to be more distortive.
- Stronger case where capital income is lightly taxed (e.g. dual income taxes with low flat rates, untaxed capital gains) or inheritance taxes are absent.
- Design: high thresholds, low rates, few exemptions, business-asset exemption with strict criteria, market-value base, limited debt deductibility, instalments for illiquid taxpayers, trust transparency, third-party reporting, international information exchange, regular evaluation.

## Limitations
Pre-dates much of the recent quasi-experimental literature (Ring 2025, Jakobsen et al. 2024). Normative conclusions depend on assumed capital-income tax systems.

## Relevance
Spain is one of the few remaining OECD countries with a net wealth tax; Spain taxes capital income in a dual system with a lower savings scale and has a heavily regionalised inheritance tax, conditions under which the OECD sees a stronger substitution role for a wealth tax.
