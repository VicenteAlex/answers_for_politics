## Question
How much do immigrants contribute in taxes and social contributions relative to the public spending they receive, compared with the native-born?

## Method and data
Static, annual accounting: every item of general-government revenue and expenditure is apportioned to foreign-born and native-born residents using labour-force and income surveys, for 25 OECD countries, 2006-2018. Variants with and without defence and public-debt charges (pure public goods), and scenarios equalising age structure and employment rates.

## Findings (from the chapter's in-brief summary as reported in search abstracts; full text not opened)
- The net fiscal position of immigrants is small in most countries, roughly between -1% and +1% of GDP over 2006-2018.
- Age is the main driver: immigrants are concentrated in working ages, which raises contributions; older immigrants contribute less.
- If prime-aged immigrants had the same employment rate as natives of the same sex and education, the net contribution would rise by more than one third of a percentage point of GDP in about 30% of countries.
- The result depends on how pure public goods (defence, debt interest) are allocated: allocating them per capita lowers immigrants' measured contribution.

## Limitations
- Static, cross-sectional: does not count future pensions of current workers or the fiscal path of their children (see study-nasem-2017-economic-fiscal-consequences-immigration for dynamic estimates).
- No causal design; no counterfactual for wages or employment of natives.
- Spain-specific figures are in annex tables not retrieved here. Graded C (careful accounting, not causal).

## Relevance
Gives the order of magnitude for "do immigrants pay their way" debates in Spain: small either way in the static annual sense. Long-run effects depend on employment rates, wages and age at arrival.
