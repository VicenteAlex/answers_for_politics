## Question
Do the labour-supply costs of extending unemployment insurance (UI) change over the business cycle?

## Method and data
Regression discontinuity at the German age thresholds that determine potential UI duration, estimated separately for each of 20 years (and by industry) and correlated with business-cycle indicators. Day-to-day administrative data on all unemployment spells and subsequent employment of mature workers. A search model with liquidity constraints maps estimates into a welfare formula based on the exhaustion rate and nonemployment effects.

## Findings
- Working paper introduction: "a moderate rise in nonemployment of about 0.1 months for each additional month of potential UI benefit duration", robust across specifications and similar across demographic groups.
- Abstract: the nonemployment effect of an extra month of UI is, at best, somewhat lower in recessions (declines mostly statistically insignificant).
- The UI exhaustion rate, and so the extra coverage provided by extensions, rises substantially in downturns; the ratio of nonemployment to benefit-duration effects is clearly countercyclical.
- Implication: the effective moral hazard of UI extensions is significantly lower in recessions than in booms.

## Limitations
Mature workers (age-based thresholds above 40); German institutions; welfare conclusions depend on the model.

## Relevance
If an AI/automation shock raises unemployment and lengthens search, this evidence suggests extending benefit duration costs relatively little in labour-supply terms in slack markets. It speaks to income insurance for displaced workers, not to reallocation itself.
